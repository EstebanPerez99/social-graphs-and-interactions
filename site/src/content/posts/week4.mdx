Every philosopher on Wikipedia's lists, born before 1900, sits in one connected web of {n(net.links)} links. We pushed a single dial until that web started to come apart. The first tradition to let go was not a modern school or an ancient one but Indian philosophy: {first.size} Vedānta, Mīmāṃsā and Buddhist-logic philosophers who were holding on by one link, {thread(first)}.

## What we asked

**If you keep only the links that matter, where does the history of philosophy snap, and who goes first?** The dial is the significance level α of the disparity filter. At α = 1 every link survives. Lower it, and a link survives only if it carries an unusually large share of the weight of at least one of its two philosophers. At some α the giant component stops being one thing. We wanted that α, the tradition that leaves, and the two philosophers whose link was the last thread.

And, as every week: **compared to what?** Maybe any network with these weights snaps at the same α, and the tradition that leaves is just whoever happens to sit on the edge. So we ran the same dial on two kinds of null network: the same links with the weights shuffled among them, and degree-preserving rewirings that carry each weight along.

## What we did

We loaded the frozen snapshot exactly as the course's snippet does: all {n(net.nodes_all)} philosophers first, then the directed links, summing the two directions into one weight. That leaves a giant component of {n(net.nodes)} philosophers and {n(net.links)} links, with weights from 1 to {net.max_weight} ({n(net.weight_one)} links have weight 1).

We implemented the disparity filter from its formula, without a package. For philosopher *i* with degree *k* and strength *s*, a link of weight *w* is significant at *i* when (1 − *w*/*s*)<sup>*k*−1</sup> < α; the link is kept if it is significant at either end. Storing the smaller of the two values per link turns every α into a simple threshold, which is what lets the page above filter in the browser. The filter reproduces the course table exactly: {n(row(0.2).links)} links and {n(row(0.2).nodes)} philosophers at α = 0.2, and the naive thresholds *w* ≥ 2, 3, 4 give 3,240, 1,572 and 785 links. (Two of those 1,540 links sit at exactly 0.2 on paper, (1 − 4/5)<sup>1</sup>. They are in the course's count only because floating point makes the number 0.19999999999999996, so the page ships the values unrounded.)

Colours come from Louvain on the full unweighted giant, not on the backbone (rule 3 of the week): {net.communities} communities, modularity *Q* = {net.q.toFixed(3)}, against {net.null_q_mean.toFixed(3)} ± {net.null_q_sd.toFixed(3)} for 20 degree-preserving shuffles (*z* ≈ {Math.round(net.z)}). Five seeds agree with each other at NMI {net.stability.nmi_min.toFixed(2)}–{net.stability.nmi_max.toFixed(2)} and find {Math.min(...net.stability.communities)} to {Math.max(...net.stability.communities)} communities, so the colours are one honest run, not the truth. We named each community after reading its strongest members.

Then the sweep: remove links from the highest α down and, at every step, check whether the giant just lost a chunk. A **break** is a chunk of {snap.method.break_size} or more philosophers leaving at once; the **last thread** is the link whose removal cut it off.

## What surprised us

**1. The first tradition to leave is Vedānta, and the Buddha stays behind.** At α = {a3(first.alpha)}, {first.size} philosophers leave together: Adi Shankara, Ramanuja, Madhvacharya and the Dvaita lineage, the Mīmāṃsā philosophers, and the Buddhist logicians Dignāga and Dharmakīrti. Their only significant link to everyone else was {thread(first)} (weight {first.bridge[0][2]}), and it was significant for the Buddha's sake: 2 of his strength of 62, not 2 of Gaudapada's 14. The Buddha himself stays in the giant, held by the Europeans who wrote about him. Read on Wikipedia, the thread is real: Gaudapada's *Māṇḍūkya Kārikā* is where the article discusses Buddhist influence on early Advaita, and two of its verses name the Buddha. Whether that influence was real is contested, which is exactly what a bridge between two traditions should look like.

**2. Real philosophy snaps four times earlier than its nulls.** With the weights shuffled across the same links, the first chunk of {snap.method.break_size} or more leaves at α = {a3(nullA.first_break_alpha_mean)} ± {a3(nullA.first_break_alpha_sd)} ({nullA.samples} networks; {nullA.no_break} never broke). With degree-preserving rewiring it leaves at {a3(nullB.first_break_alpha_mean)} ± {a3(nullB.first_break_alpha_sd)}. The observed {a3(first.alpha)} is above every one of the 100 nulls. And the null chunks are a mix: on average only {Math.round(100 * purity(nullA.rows))} % (shuffled weights) and {Math.round(100 * purity(nullB.rows))} % (rewired) of a null chunk shares one community, against {Math.round(100 * first.purity)} % for the real one. The early break is not the degree sequence and not the weight distribution. It is *where* the weights sit: heavy inside traditions, light across them.

**3. Philosophy loses its majority through Voltaire.** The second break is {tradition(second)}: {second.size} philosophers from Confucius and Mencius to Zhu Xi and Wang Yangming leave at α = {a3(second.alpha)}, and on that same step the giant falls from over half of the network to {n(second.giant_after)} philosophers. The last thread is {thread(second)} (weight {second.bridge[0][2]}), significant on Voltaire's side. That is a bridge the Enlightenment built on purpose: Voltaire admired Confucian ethics and government and said so often, and Confucius's article lists him among his Western readers.

**4. The threads are Wikipedia's history of philosophy, not philosophy's.** A link means one article mentions another, and the weight counts mentions. The very first chunk to leave, at α = {a3(snap.events[0].alpha)}, is six Upanishadic sages, and all {snap.events[0].bridge.length} of their last threads ran through {hub(snap.events[0])}, the Victorian scholar who translated the Upanishads. That is a true fact about how the English-speaking world met these texts, and a reminder that "the East leaves first" is partly a statement about which articles English Wikipedia writes densely and which it links sparingly.

## What we would check next

The colours are one Louvain run; we would run Infomap on the same giant and check whether the islands the dial finds are the same islands a flow-based method finds. We would also rerun the sweep with weighted Louvain colours, and ask the dual question: at what α does each *community* fragment internally, rather than when it leaves the giant? Vedānta leaving as one block suggests it is unusually cohesive; the European communities, which never leave cleanly, may fall apart from the inside first.
